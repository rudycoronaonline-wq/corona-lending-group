SECTION 3: WHAT IS A COMMERCIAL LOAN

What Is a Commercial Loan?

A commercial loan is financing secured by a property that is used primarily for business or investment purposes, rather than as a personal residence. Unlike residential mortgages, commercial loans are evaluated primarily on the performance of the property and the strength of the business or borrower behind it.

Commercial loans are used to:

Purchase an office building, retail center, warehouse, or mixed-use property

Refinance an existing commercial mortgage to lower the rate or access equity

Finance the construction of a new commercial building from the ground up

Fund renovations or improvements to an existing commercial property

Acquire an owner-occupied commercial property where the borrower's business operates

Purchase multi-family apartment buildings with 5 or more units

The commercial lending landscape is meaningfully different from residential. Loan terms, rates, underwriting criteria, and documentation requirements all work differently. That is why working with an advisor who understands commercial financing is so important.

SECTION 6: HOW COMMERCIAL LOANS ARE UNDERWRITTEN

How Lenders Evaluate a Commercial Loan

Commercial loan underwriting is fundamentally different from residential underwriting. Understanding what lenders look at gives you a meaningful advantage when you approach a deal.

The 5 Pillars of Commercial Underwriting

1. Net Operating Income (NOI)

NOI is the property's gross income minus all operating expenses, before debt service. It is the foundation of commercial underwriting because it tells the lender how much cash the property actually generates.

NOI = Gross Rental Income minus Vacancy and Credit Loss minus Operating Expenses (taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, utilities)

The higher the NOI, the more debt the property can support.

2. Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR)

DSCR measures how much NOI is available to cover the annual loan payment. Lenders want to see that a property generates meaningfully more income than it costs to carry.

DSCR = Net Operating Income / Annual Debt Service (Principal + Interest)

3. Loan-to-Value Ratio (LTV)

LTV is the loan amount divided by the property's appraised value. It tells the lender how much equity cushion exists. Lower LTV means more equity and lower risk.

4. Borrower Financial Strength

Even in asset-based commercial underwriting, the borrower still matters. Lenders evaluate:

Credit score: 680+ for most programs; 700+ for best terms; 720+ for agency multifamily

Global cash flow: Your total income from all sources compared to all debt obligations

Net worth: Many lenders want borrower net worth equal to or greater than the loan amount

Liquidity reserves: Post-closing cash reserves (typically 6 to 12 months of PITI)

Commercial real estate experience: Prior ownership, management, or investment experience adds confidence

5. Property and Market Analysis

The lender will review the property itself and the market it sits in:

Commercial appraisal: Determines market value using income approach, sales comparison, and cost approach

Environmental assessment (Phase I): Required on most commercial transactions to identify contamination risk

Property condition report / PCA: Documents the physical condition and deferred maintenance

Rent roll and lease analysis: Review of all existing leases, terms, expiration dates, and tenant quality

Market vacancy and absorption rates: How the local market is performing for this property type

Understanding these five pillars before you approach a lender puts you in a much stronger position. I help clients review their deal through a lender's lens before we ever submit an application, so we identify and address any weaknesses on our terms, not the lender's.

SECTION 10: THE COMMERCIAL LOAN PROCESS

How the Commercial Loan Process Works with Me

Commercial deals have more moving parts than residential transactions. Here is exactly what to expect from start to close, and how I manage each phase on your behalf.

Phase 1: Deal Analysis and Strategy (Week 1)

Before I place your loan with any lender, I want to understand your deal completely. We will go through:

The property: Type, location, condition, current income, and occupancy

Your goals: Purchase, refinance, cash-out, construction, or stabilization

Your financial profile: Credit, income, net worth, liquidity, and experience

Deal structure: Purchase price or appraised value, desired loan amount, equity position

From this conversation, I determine which programs you qualify for, what the likely loan terms look like, and what, if anything, needs to be addressed before we move forward. I give you a realistic picture, not a sales pitch.

Phase 2: Lender Matching and Term Sheet (Weeks 1 to 3)

Commercial lending is not commoditized. Different lenders specialize in different property types, loan sizes, and borrower profiles. I match your deal to the lenders who are most likely to approve it and offer the best terms.

Once I have identified the right lender, we submit a loan package. A strong commercial loan package includes:

Executive summary of the deal and borrower

Rent roll and lease abstracts (for income-producing properties)

Last 2 to 3 years of property operating statements

Purchase contract or refinance payoff statement

Borrower personal financial statement and tax returns

Business financial statements (if applicable)

Photos and property details

The lender reviews the package and issues a Term Sheet or Letter of Intent, outlining the proposed loan amount, rate, terms, and conditions. I negotiate these terms on your behalf before you accept.

Phase 3: Due Diligence and Appraisal (Weeks 3 to 8)

Once you accept the term sheet, the lender opens formal due diligence. This is the most documentation-intensive phase and typically includes:

Commercial appraisal ordered and completed by an MAI-certified appraiser

Phase I environmental assessment

Property condition report (PCA) if required

Title search and preliminary title report

Lease review and estoppel certificates from tenants

Survey (if required)

Additional borrower documentation requested by underwriting

I stay in active contact with the lender, the appraisers, and the title company throughout this phase to keep things moving and handle issues as they arise.

Phase 4: Underwriting and Commitment (Weeks 6 to 10)

The lender's credit committee reviews the full due diligence package and issues a formal loan commitment letter. This letter specifies all final loan terms and any remaining conditions that must be satisfied before closing.

I review the commitment letter with you line by line. Anything that needs negotiation or clarification gets handled before you sign.

Phase 5: Closing (Weeks 10 to 14)

Commercial closings are coordinated between the lender's counsel, your attorney, the title and escrow company, and in some cases the SBA or other agencies. I coordinate across all parties to keep the process moving toward a clean close.

At closing, you sign the loan documents, funds are disbursed, and the property transfers or the refinance is completed.

Total timeline from application to close typically ranges from 45 to 90 days depending on loan type, property complexity, and lender pipeline. SBA loans can sometimes take longer due to government processing.

SECTION 14: FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Commercial Loan Frequently Asked Questions

What is the minimum down payment for a commercial loan?

It depends on the program. Conventional commercial loans typically require 20% to 30% down. SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 loans can go as low as 10% for eligible owner-occupied properties. DSCR commercial loans typically require 25% to 35%. Bridge loans are generally based on LTV rather than a stated down payment percentage, and the equity requirement varies. I will identify the program that gets you to the table with the least capital while still meeting your goals.

Can I get a commercial loan if I am self-employed?

Yes, and commercial lending is actually often more accessible for self-employed borrowers than residential financing. DSCR commercial loans qualify based on the property's income, not your personal income, which removes the challenge of write-offs reducing your qualifying income on paper. SBA loans do require business and personal income documentation, but they are accustomed to evaluating self-employed businesses. I work with self-employed borrowers regularly and know how to present your file to the right lender.

What credit score do I need for a commercial loan?

Most conventional commercial lenders want to see a credit score of 680 or higher. SBA programs generally require 650 to 680 minimum, with stronger scores opening better terms. DSCR commercial loans are more flexible, with some programs allowing scores in the 620 to 650 range if the property cash flow is strong. The property and the deal can compensate for credit that is not perfect, but we need to be honest about where yours stands before we start.

How long does it take to close a commercial loan?

The typical commercial loan takes 45 to 90 days from application to close, depending on the loan type, property, and lender. Conventional deals with a straightforward property often close in 45 to 60 days. SBA loans can take 60 to 90 days or more due to government processing. Bridge loans are designed to close quickly, sometimes in as little as 2 to 3 weeks. I will give you a realistic timeline estimate for your specific deal early in the process.

Can I use a commercial loan to purchase a mixed-use property?

Yes. Mixed-use properties, those with both commercial and residential components, are eligible for commercial financing. The underwriting considers the income from both the commercial and residential portions. If the residential component makes up the majority of the income, some residential programs may also be worth exploring. I will review the specific property and determine the best path.

What is a balloon payment and do I have to worry about it?

A balloon payment is a lump sum payment of the remaining principal due at the end of the loan term. Most commercial loans have them. For example, a loan might have a 5-year term with a 25-year amortization schedule, meaning you make payments as if the loan runs 25 years, but at the end of year 5, the remaining balance is due in full. You either refinance or sell at that point. This is normal in commercial lending, but it does require you to plan ahead. I always make sure my clients understand their balloon structure and have a strategy for it before they close.

Can I refinance a commercial property I already own?

Absolutely. Commercial refinancing is one of the most common transactions I work on. You might refinance to get a lower rate, to pull cash equity out for a new investment, to pay off a balloon coming due, or to move from a bridge loan to permanent financing. The process is similar to a purchase, but without the purchase contract. Bring me your current loan details and a recent rent roll, and I will tell you what your refinance options look like.

What is the difference between recourse and non-recourse commercial loans?

A recourse loan means the lender can pursue your personal assets if the property income does not cover the debt and the lender suffers a loss. A non-recourse loan limits the lender's recovery to the property itself. Most conventional commercial and SBA loans are full recourse, meaning you sign a personal guarantee. Agency multifamily loans, particularly from Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, can be non-recourse for qualifying borrowers. Non-recourse typically requires a stronger deal and a larger equity position. I will always make sure you understand what you are personally guaranteeing before you sign.
