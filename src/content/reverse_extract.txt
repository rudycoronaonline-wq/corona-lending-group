SECTION 3: WHAT IS A REVERSE MORTGAGE
What Is a Reverse Mortgage?
A reverse mortgage is a government-backed home loan available to homeowners aged 62 or older that allows you to convert a portion of your home equity into tax-free funds, without selling your home, giving up ownership, or making monthly mortgage payments.
The most widely used reverse mortgage is the Home Equity Conversion Mortgage, or HECM, which is insured by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) and regulated by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). There are also proprietary reverse mortgages offered by private lenders for higher-value properties.
Here is the key concept that surprises most people: instead of you paying the lender each month, the lender pays you. The loan balance grows over time as interest accrues, and it is repaid when you sell the home, permanently move out, or pass away.
You keep full ownership of your home. Your name stays on the title. The bank does not own your house. That is one of the most common myths I hear, and it is simply not true.
Think of a reverse mortgage not as taking something away from your home, but as unlocking what your home has already earned for you over a lifetime.

SECTION 5: HOW YOU RECEIVE THE FUNDS
Five Ways to Receive Your Money
One of the most powerful features of a reverse mortgage is the flexibility in how you receive funds. You choose the option that fits your life, not the other way around.
1. Lump Sum (Fixed-Rate HECMs Only)
Receive all available funds at once at closing. This is the only fixed-rate option in the HECM program. It works well for borrowers who need to pay off an existing mortgage, cover a large medical expense, or want to invest the funds with a financial advisor.
Best for: Paying off a large debt or existing mortgage immediately
Rate type: Fixed only
Consideration: You receive all funds upfront, so interest accrues on the full balance from day one
2. Tenure Payments (Adjustable-Rate HECMs)
Receive equal monthly payments for as long as you live in the home as your primary residence. Even if you outlive your projected loan balance, payments continue. This functions like a private pension funded by your home equity.
Best for: Supplementing a fixed monthly income for life
Rate type: Adjustable
Consideration: Payments stop if you permanently leave the home
3. Term Payments (Adjustable-Rate HECMs)
Receive equal monthly payments for a set number of years that you choose. This gives you higher monthly amounts than tenure payments for a defined period.
Best for: Covering expenses during a specific window, such as a few years before Social Security kicks in
Rate type: Adjustable
Consideration: Payments stop at end of term; line of credit continues
4. Line of Credit (Most Popular Option)
Access funds as needed, whenever you need them, up to your available principal limit. Any unused portion of the line of credit grows over time at the same rate as the interest on the loan. This growth is guaranteed, regardless of what happens to your home's value.
Best for: Flexibility and long-term planning; accessing funds only when needed
Rate type: Adjustable
Consideration: This is the option most financial planners recommend for strategic retirement planning
The growing line of credit is one of the most powerful and underused features in retirement planning. Many financial advisors now recommend establishing a HECM line of credit early, even if you do not need the funds yet, because the available credit grows over time regardless of home values.
5. Combination
Mix and match. Receive a partial lump sum at closing, set up monthly tenure or term payments, and keep a remaining line of credit. This is the most customized approach and works well for borrowers with multiple financial goals.
Best for: Paying off existing mortgage with lump sum while setting up monthly income and keeping a reserve
Rate type: Adjustable

SECTION 9: WHEN DOES THE LOAN COME DUE
When Does the Loan Need to Be Repaid?
This is one of the most important things to understand, and I want to walk you through it clearly.
The reverse mortgage becomes due and payable when any one of the following triggering events occurs:
Triggering Events
You sell the home
You permanently move out of the home (including moving to an assisted living facility for more than 12 consecutive months)
The last surviving borrower passes away
You fail to maintain the home as your primary residence
You fall behind on property taxes or homeowners insurance (and do not cure the default)
The home falls into disrepair and you fail to make required repairs
What Happens When the Loan Is Due?
Your heirs or estate will typically have 6 months to resolve the loan, with possible extensions up to 12 months. Options include:
Sell the home: Use the proceeds to pay off the reverse mortgage balance. Any equity remaining after repayment goes directly to the heirs.
Refinance: Heirs can take out a conventional mortgage to pay off the reverse mortgage balance and keep the home.
Pay off the balance directly: If heirs have other assets, they can pay the loan balance and retain the home.
Deed in lieu: If the loan balance exceeds the home value, heirs can simply hand the keys to the lender with no additional liability.
This is the non-recourse protection: you or your heirs will NEVER owe more than the home is worth at the time of sale. If the loan balance exceeds the sale price, FHA insurance covers the difference. Your other assets, savings, and estate are fully protected.
SECTION 10: NON-BORROWING SPOUSE PROTECTIONS
What Happens to My Spouse Who Is Under 62?
This is a question I hear often, and it is an important one. If one spouse is 62 or older and the other is younger, the younger spouse can be designated as a Non-Borrowing Spouse (NBS) on the loan.
Under current HUD rules, a Non-Borrowing Spouse has the right to remain in the home after the borrowing spouse passes away or permanently moves out, as long as:
The home is the Non-Borrowing Spouse's principal residence
The Non-Borrowing Spouse was married to the borrower at loan closing and remains so
The Non-Borrowing Spouse continues to pay property taxes, insurance, and maintain the home
The Non-Borrowing Spouse was properly disclosed at the time of application
However, there is an important trade-off: the principal limit is calculated based on the younger spouse's age, which reduces the loan amount compared to calculating it on the older spouse alone. I will always run both scenarios so you can make an informed decision.
Non-Borrowing Spouse rules have changed significantly over the past decade in favor of protection. If you researched this years ago and were concerned, the rules today are meaningfully better for couples. Let's have that conversation.
SECTION 11: MYTHS VS. FACTS
Reverse Mortgage Myths vs. Reality
I hear the same concerns from families across Southern California. Let me address the most common ones head on.
The Myth
The Reality
"The bank takes ownership of my home."
False. You retain full title and ownership. The lender places a lien on the property, just like any mortgage. Your name stays on the deed.
"My kids will inherit debt."
False. HECMs are non-recourse loans. Heirs cannot inherit a debt larger than the home's value. They can sell, refinance, or walk away with zero liability beyond the home.
"Reverse mortgages are a last resort for desperate people."
False. Many financial planners actively recommend reverse mortgages as part of a retirement income strategy, particularly the line of credit option, to preserve investments and manage cash flow.
"I will get kicked out of my home."
False. As long as you live in the home, maintain it, and keep taxes and insurance current, you cannot be forced out by the lender.
"The loan is only for people with no money."
False. Reverse mortgages serve homeowners across all income levels. Many affluent retirees use them for strategic cash flow management rather than from financial need.
"My heirs will hate me for doing this."
Not necessarily. A reverse mortgage that reduces your financial stress, covers medical costs, or allows you to maintain your lifestyle preserves your dignity and can actually reduce the burden on your children.
"I cannot get a reverse mortgage if I still have a mortgage."
False. You can have an existing mortgage. It must be paid off at closing, but you can use the reverse mortgage proceeds to do exactly that.

SECTION 14: THE HUD COUNSELING PROCESS
The Required HUD Counseling Session
Before you can apply for a HECM reverse mortgage, federal law requires you to complete an independent counseling session with a HUD-approved housing counselor. This is not optional, and it is not something I provide. It is a third-party session designed to make sure you truly understand what you are getting into.
I actually think this is one of the best protections in the entire program. I want my clients to go into this decision with every question answered.
What to Expect
Session length: Typically 60 to 90 minutes by phone or in person
Cost: Usually $125 to $175, paid directly to the counselor
Topics covered: How HECMs work, costs and benefits, alternatives, your rights, your obligations
You will receive: A HECM Counseling Certificate (required for your loan application)
Timeline: Certificate is valid for 180 days from the date of counseling
How to Find a HUD-Approved Counselor

Step 2: HUD Counseling (Days 3 to 7)
Before I can process your application, you complete your required HUD counseling session. I will help you find a counselor and prepare you with the right questions to ask. You will receive your counseling certificate, which is valid for 180 days.
Step 3: Formal Application (Days 7 to 10)
Once you decide to move forward, I gather your application documents. For a reverse mortgage, documentation is simpler than a conventional loan but still thorough:
Government-issued photo ID
Social Security card or proof of Social Security number
Proof of homeownership (deed)
Most recent mortgage statement (if applicable)
Proof of property taxes current status
Proof of homeowners insurance
Income documentation: Social Security award letter, pension statements, retirement account statements
HUD counseling certificate
Step 4: Home Appraisal (Days 10 to 20)
An FHA-approved appraiser visits the home to determine its current market value and condition. This sets the baseline for your loan amount. If the home needs repairs, the appraiser will note them. Some repairs can be addressed after closing using a set-aside from your loan funds.
Step 5: Underwriting and Approval (Days 20 to 35)
The lender's underwriter reviews your full file, the appraisal, and the title report. They confirm eligibility and issue final loan approval. I stay in close contact with you throughout this phase and handle any conditions that come up.
Step 6: Closing Day
You attend a closing meeting, typically at a title or escrow company. A notary walks you through signing the loan documents. There is a 3-day right of rescission period after signing, during which you can cancel with no penalty. After the rescission period ends, funds are disbursed according to your chosen payment option.
From application to close, most reverse mortgages take 30 to 45 days. I will keep you informed every step of the way.

SECTION 17: REVERSE MORTGAGE FOR PURCHASE (HECM FOR PURCHASE)
Did You Know You Can Use a Reverse Mortgage to BUY a Home?
Most people do not know this option exists. A HECM for Purchase allows eligible borrowers 62 and older to purchase a new primary residence using a combination of their own funds and reverse mortgage proceeds, with no required monthly mortgage payments on the new home.
How It Works
You find a home you want to purchase.
You make a down payment from your own funds (typically 40% to 60% of the purchase price, depending on your age and current rates).
The reverse mortgage covers the remainder.
You move in, no monthly mortgage payment required.
Who Uses HECM for Purchase?
Seniors selling a larger family home and buying a smaller, more manageable home
Retirees relocating to be closer to family or in a better climate
Buyers who want to preserve liquid assets rather than tie up cash in a new home
Anyone 62+ who wants to buy a new home without taking on a monthly mortgage payment
Example: A 70-year-old sells their home in one city and wants to buy a $500,000 home closer to family. Rather than paying all cash, they put down approximately $250,000 and use a HECM for Purchase to cover the rest. They move in with no monthly mortgage payment, and they preserve $250,000 in liquid reserves.

SECTION 19: FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS
Reverse Mortgage Frequently Asked Questions
Does the bank own my home with a reverse mortgage?
No. You retain full ownership and title to your home throughout the life of the loan. The lender places a lien on the property as collateral, exactly like any other mortgage. Your name stays on the deed and you have every right of ownership.
What if my loan balance grows larger than my home's value?
This is called going underwater, and it is protected by FHA insurance on HECM loans. Because reverse mortgages are non-recourse loans, you or your heirs will never owe more than the home's fair market value at the time of sale. If the loan exceeds the value, FHA covers the shortfall. No other assets are touched.
Can I still leave my home to my children?
Yes. When you pass away, your heirs have options. They can sell the home, use the proceeds to repay the reverse mortgage balance, and keep whatever equity remains. Or they can refinance into a conventional mortgage and keep the home. The key is that any remaining equity, after the loan is repaid, belongs entirely to your heirs.
Do I have to make monthly payments?
No monthly mortgage payments are required. That is one of the defining features of a reverse mortgage. You are still responsible for property taxes, homeowners insurance, and maintaining the home, but there is no monthly loan payment.
What if I want to sell my home after getting a reverse mortgage?
You can sell at any time. There are no prepayment penalties. When you sell, the reverse mortgage balance is paid off from the proceeds, and any remaining equity is yours.
Will a reverse mortgage affect my Social Security or Medicare?
Reverse mortgage proceeds are loan funds, not income, so they do not affect Social Security or Medicare benefits. However, if you receive Medicaid or Supplemental Security Income (SSI), large amounts of liquid funds can affect eligibility. I always recommend consulting with a financial advisor or benefits counselor about your specific benefit situation before proceeding.
What if I need to move to a care facility?
If you move to a care facility temporarily, for less than 12 consecutive months, the loan remains intact. If the move becomes permanent, the loan becomes due. Your heirs or estate would then have 6 months (with possible extension to 12) to sell, refinance, or resolve the loan. No-borrowing spouse protections may also apply if applicable.
Can I get a reverse mortgage if I currently have a mortgage?
Yes. You can have an existing mortgage, home equity loan, or HELOC. All existing liens must be paid off at closing, but you can use your reverse mortgage proceeds to do so. In fact, many borrowers use a reverse mortgage specifically to eliminate their current monthly mortgage payment.
How is the interest rate determined?
HECM adjustable-rate loans use the Constant Maturity Treasury (CMT) index plus a margin set by the lender. Fixed-rate HECMs use a fixed rate set at closing. Interest accrues monthly on the outstanding loan balance but is not due until the loan is repaid. I will show you current rates and projections in our consultation.
Is the money I receive taxable?
No. Reverse mortgage proceeds are loan advances, not income, so they are not subject to federal or California income tax. However, I always recommend consulting with a CPA or tax advisor about your complete financial situation.
