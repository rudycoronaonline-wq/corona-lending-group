SECTION 3: WHY SELF-EMPLOYED BORROWERS STRUGGLE

Why the Traditional Mortgage System Fails Self-Employed Borrowers

Before we talk about solutions, I want to name the problem clearly. Because the self-employed borrower's challenge is not unique to their situation. It is a structural flaw in the way most lenders are set up.

The Tax Return Problem

Conventional mortgages use your federal tax returns to establish qualifying income. Specifically, they look at your adjusted gross income after all deductions and business expenses. For a smart business owner who correctly writes off depreciation, home office expenses, vehicle use, equipment, and other legitimate deductions, that taxable income can be a fraction of what you actually bring home.

Example: A business owner genuinely earns $250,000 per year in gross business revenue. After legitimate business expenses and write-offs, their tax returns show $85,000 in taxable income. A conventional lender uses $85,000 to calculate qualifying power. The real earning capacity of $250,000 is invisible to them.

The Two-Year Requirement

Conventional lenders require two full years of self-employment history to count your business income. If you left a corporate job 18 months ago to start something successful, that income likely does not count. If you recently transitioned from a side business to a full-time business, the same problem applies.

Income Variability

Self-employed income often varies from month to month and year to year. A good Q4 might be offset by a slower Q1. Conventional lenders average your last two years of income, which can produce a qualifying number that does not represent your current trajectory at all. If your income grew significantly in the most recent year, averaging may actually hurt you.

Multiple Income Streams

Many self-employed borrowers have income from multiple sources: a primary business, 1099 contract work, rental properties, dividends, and other streams. The conventional underwriting process struggles with complexity, often disallowing income sources it cannot easily verify through traditional documentation.

The good news is that the mortgage industry has evolved to address all of these challenges. There are now multiple loan programs specifically designed for self-employed borrowers. I have access to all of them.

What Deposits Count and What Does Not

Lenders review every deposit in your statements and classify them. Not all deposits are counted as qualifying income:

Business deposits from clients, customers, or revenue sources: Count

Direct transfers from a business account to your personal account (owner draws): Count

Consistent contract payments or consulting fees: Count

Transfers between your own accounts (moving money from savings to checking): Do not count as income

Large one-time deposits that are not business-related: Do not count; must be explained

Loan proceeds or gift funds deposited: Do not count as income

Insurance proceeds or lawsuit settlements: Do not count as income

SECTION 8: SOLVING THE WRITE-OFF PROBLEM

The Write-Off Problem: How We Work Around It

This is the single biggest frustration I hear from self-employed borrowers. You did everything right. You ran your business well. You worked with a good CPA who helped you minimize your tax liability. And now the mortgage lender is telling you that your income is too low to qualify.

Let me explain what is actually happening and how we solve it.

How Conventional Lenders Calculate Self-Employed Income

For a conventional loan, the lender uses your Schedule C (sole proprietor), Schedule K-1 (S-Corp/LLC partnership), or Form 1120-S (S-Corp), adds back certain non-cash deductions like depreciation, and arrives at an adjusted qualifying income. The lower your taxable income after deductions, the lower your qualifying power with a conventional lender.

This is not the lender being difficult. It is how the Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines work. They require tax-return-based income verification for conventional loans, and there is no flexibility built into those guidelines for alternative documentation.

The Solution: Alternative Documentation Programs

SECTION 9: PLANNING AHEAD - TALK TO ME BEFORE YOUR CPA

The Most Important Advice I Can Give You: Talk to Me Before Tax Season

This section might be the most valuable thing on this entire page. So I want you to read it carefully.

If you are planning to buy a home or investment property in the next 12 to 24 months, talk to me before you sit down with your CPA to prepare your taxes.

Why This Matters

Every business deduction your CPA takes on your tax return reduces your taxable income. That is exactly what a good CPA is supposed to do. But if you are planning to buy a home using conventional financing, every deduction that reduces your taxable income also reduces your qualifying income in the eyes of the lender.

There is a direct trade-off between tax savings and mortgage qualifying power. The question is how to optimize both.

Scenario

Mortgage Impact

The Strategic Approach

I am not telling you to pay more taxes than you owe. I am telling you to understand the trade-off before tax season happens. In some cases, it makes sense to pull back on certain deductions in the year before you plan to buy, maximize your qualifying income, close on your home, and then return to an aggressive deduction strategy the following year. In other cases, using a bank statement loan makes more sense and you keep your full deduction strategy intact.

I have had this conversation with many business owners. A few of them called me after their taxes were already filed and their income was already locked. Others called me three months before tax season and we were able to plan for an outcome that served both goals. Call me before you file. The conversation costs nothing and could save you thousands.

SECTION 11: MYTHS VS. REALITY FOR SELF-EMPLOYED BORROWERS

Myths vs. Reality: What Self-Employed Borrowers Are Often Told

The Myth

The Reality

"You need 2 years of W-2 income to get a mortgage"

You need 2 years of self-employment history for most programs, but there are programs for even shorter histories under the right conditions. And there are programs that never look at employment at all (DSCR).

"Your write-offs will disqualify you"

Only if you are using a conventional full-doc program. Bank statement loans use your actual deposits, not your taxable income. Your write-offs become irrelevant.

"Self-employed borrowers cannot get competitive rates"

Alternative doc programs do carry slightly higher rates than conventional (0.5% to 1.5% typically), but they are nowhere near hard money or predatory products. The rate premium is the cost of flexibility, and it is often worth it.

"You have to put 20% or 30% down"

Bank statement loans are available with as little as 10% down for primary residences. The down payment requirement varies by program and credit score.

"Lenders will deny anyone with complex income"

The right lender, with the right program, is specifically set up to handle complex income. The problem is not complexity. The problem is submitting to the wrong lender.

"You have to show 2 years of business tax returns no matter what"

Bank statement loans, 1099 loans, P&L loans, and DSCR loans all have paths that do not require business tax returns. Some do not require any tax returns at all.

"Self-employed borrowers always pay higher rates"

Only on alternative doc programs. If your tax return income qualifies for a conventional loan, you get conventional rates. Rates are program-specific, not tied to employment type.

SECTION 13: THE SELF-EMPLOYED LOAN PROCESS

How the Self-Employed Loan Process Works with Me

Working with a self-employed borrower requires more upfront strategy than a standard W-2 file. Here is exactly how I approach every self-employed client from the first conversation to closing day.

Step 1: Income Analysis and Program Selection (Week 1)

Before we submit anything to any lender, I want to understand your complete financial picture. We will go through:

How your business is structured: LLC, S-Corp, sole proprietor, partnership, 1099 contractor

What your last 12 to 24 months of bank statements look like: deposit volume, consistency, trends

What your most recent two years of tax returns show if you have them

What property you want to buy and whether it is a primary, second home, or investment

Your credit score range and current debt profile

From this conversation, I identify which program produces the highest qualifying income for your specific situation. I run the numbers on every applicable program and show you which one gives you the best outcome.

SECTION 16: FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Self-Employed Loan Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to use my tax returns to get a mortgage if I am self-employed?

No. While conventional loans require tax returns, multiple alternative documentation programs exist specifically for self-employed borrowers: bank statement loans, 1099 loans, P&L loans, and DSCR loans for investment properties. Each of these programs qualifies your income through alternative documentation and does not rely on your tax return adjusted gross income. The right program depends on your specific income profile.

How long do I need to have been self-employed?

Most alternative documentation programs require 2 full years of self-employment history. Some programs accept 1 year under specific conditions. Your self-employment history is documented through your business license or registration date, CPA letters, business bank account history, and sometimes professional license records. If you are newer to self-employment, let us talk about what your specific timeline looks like and what options may exist.

My tax returns show very low income because of deductions. How much can I actually qualify for?

This depends entirely on which program we use and what your bank statements or other documentation shows. I have seen borrowers whose tax returns show $65,000 in taxable income qualify for significantly higher loan amounts through bank statement programs based on actual deposit history reflecting $180,000 or more in annual deposits. The only way to know your number is to run it. That is the first thing I do in our consultation.

Can I use personal bank statements or do I need business bank statements?

Both are accepted by most bank statement lenders. Personal statements are simpler because every deposit is counted without an expense ratio. Business statements require an expense ratio adjustment. If your business income flows primarily through a personal account, personal statements may be more favorable. If most of your revenue runs through a business account, the business statement approach is standard. I will review both and recommend the path that produces the higher qualifying income.

My business had a great year this year but a slower year last year. Which year does the lender use?

For conventional full-doc loans, lenders average the last two years of tax return income. If your most recent year was significantly better, this average hurts you. For bank statement programs, some lenders allow 12-month statements only, which captures just your most recent year. Others use 24 months. Choosing the 12-month option when your recent income is your strongest can meaningfully increase your qualifying income. I will always review both periods and recommend which works best for your situation.

I just became self-employed 14 months ago after leaving a corporate job. Can I qualify?

It depends. Most programs require 24 months of self-employment. However, there are situations where a borrower with 12 months of documented self-employment can qualify, particularly if they are in the same field as their prior W-2 employment and can demonstrate stable or growing income. FHA has some flexibility here. Some bank statement lenders have 1-year programs. Let us look at your specific situation. There may be a path.

What is an expense ratio and how does it affect what I qualify for?

When using business bank statements, lenders apply an expense ratio to estimate what portion of your deposits represents profit versus pass-through business expenses. For example, a 50% expense ratio means the lender counts 50% of your gross deposits as qualifying income. The standard ratio varies by business type, from 50% for service businesses to 70-80% for product-based businesses. A CPA-prepared expense statement documenting your actual expenses can sometimes result in a more favorable ratio if your real expenses are lower than the standard.

Can I use a bank statement loan to buy a primary residence?

Yes. Bank statement loans are available for primary residences, second homes, and investment properties. Down payment requirements for primary residences can be as low as 10%, depending on your credit score and the specific lender. Second homes typically require 10% to 15% down. Investment properties typically require 20% to 25%.

Will my interest rate be much higher than a conventional loan?

Alternative documentation programs typically carry rates that are 0.5% to 1.5% higher than comparable conventional loans. This is the cost of the flexibility the program provides. The exact rate premium depends on your credit score, down payment, loan amount, and which program you use. On a $600,000 loan, a 1% rate difference is approximately $350 per month. For many self-employed borrowers, having the right loan to begin with is worth more than chasing the lowest possible rate on a program they cannot qualify for.

Can I get a bank statement loan in my LLC?

DSCR investment property loans are widely available through LLCs and are the standard approach for investors. Bank statement loans for primary residences typically require individual borrowing, not entity borrowing. Some specialty lenders may allow LLC borrowing on bank statement loans for investment or second home properties. I will clarify which entity options are available for your specific loan type when we discuss your situation.
